# Techniques

These order your thinking. They are not headings on the page. Do not print “Problem / Agitate / Solution.” Do not stamp one frame on every catalog row.

## Who already wants this (Schwartz)

You point at a want they have. You do not invent a category in the hero.

| They know | Open with | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nothing about you | The problem they already feel | “Your crawler dies after 20 minutes” |
| The problem | Stakes | Bans, retries, wasted proxy spend |
| The kind of fix | How it works | Sticky residential vs ISP vs unlocker API |
| The product | Name + deal | Price, trial, what is gated, who should skip |
| The deal | Mechanics | Coupon, plan math, migration |

A visitor who already knows the product does not need “reimagine connectivity.” They need the mechanism and the limit.

## Pick one frame per piece

**PAS** — Problem, agitate, solution. Agitate with time, money, or risk. Not melodrama.

**AIDA** — Attention, interest, desire, action. Headlines and ads. Attention is a claim, not a mood.

**BAB** — Before, after, bridge. The after-state must be checkable. Not “unlock potential.”

**FAB** — Feature → advantage → benefit. Every feature needs “so you can…”. Orphan features go.

**4 U’s** — Urgent (only if true), unique, useful, ultra-specific. Vague copy fails useful and specific.

**4 C’s** (review) — Clear, short, worth reading, believable. Fail clear or believable and it does not go out.

**Bencivenga check** — Promise + proof + offer + real urgency, minus friction. A promise with no proof is a lie the reader smells.

## Headlines and hero

One H1. Same promise as the title. Not a keyword list.

In five seconds, H1 plus subhead should say what it is, who it is for, and why care (CXL). Ogilvy: one promise.

If they searched for an outcome, lead with the outcome. If they searched the product, lead with name and deal.

Do not open the H1 on “Don’t miss…” unless that is already how this brand talks.

A fact that implies the benefit beats a clever line. Ogilvy’s Rolls-Royce ad did that with cabin noise at 60 mph.

## Proof

Hopkins and Ogilvy: a number beats an adjective. Put a figure, a named source, a limit, or a skip-condition above the fold when you have one.

Cialdini, used honestly:

- Proof: name, role, company, a result. “Jane S., happy customer” is not proof.
- Authority: credentials you can check.
- Scarcity: a real date, stock, or cohort cap. A banner that says “limited time” every month is a lie.
- Similarity: people like the buyer, not a celebrity who would never use this.

No quote, logo, “#1,” or price you cannot source. Missing? Omit.

## Buttons

One main button. The second one is quieter.

The label is the action plus the result (`Start 14-day trial`). Not `Submit`. Not `Learn more` as the only words.

On a buy page, price is visible or one click away. Two equal buttons in the hero split the decision.

## Compare and catalog

Criteria first, then the vendor. Say who should skip (“five dedicated IPs? not this plan”).

If the decision is a sentence, write the sentence.

Each entity page starts from different facts, so it gets a different outline. Use FAB when you are listing what it does. Use PAS only when that vendor’s story is a pain story.

## Email and ads

Short. One offer. The subject does the 4 U’s. The body does not retell the homepage. End on the ask.

## Apps and docs

The frames above are for pages that sell. UI and docs have another job. See [surfaces.md](surfaces.md).

UI: clear, then short, then a bit of personality if it does not block the task. The command names the next state. Errors say what happened and how to fix it. No blame. STE limits.

Docs: tutorial, how-to, reference, and explanation live on separate pages. Put the condition before the step. Task headings start with a bare infinitive (`Create an instance`). STE: 20-word steps. Plain words. Define a hard term once.

## Frames gone wrong

Do not stack AIDA, then PAS, then BAB in one article.

Do not give every CMS row the same three H2s “because PAS.”

Do not teach the frame to the visitor (“Using the PAS model…”).
